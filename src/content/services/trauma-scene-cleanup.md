## What trauma scene cleanup actually involves

Trauma scene cleanup covers the removal and decontamination of biological material following a death, violent incident, or serious accident. The work goes beyond visible cleaning. Affected materials such as carpet padding, drywall, and porous flooring often have to be removed entirely because blood and other bodily fluids can penetrate deeper than surface cleaning can reach. Crews use personal protective equipment, hospital-grade disinfectants, and sealed containers rated for biological waste. Depending on the scope, a job can run from a few hours for a contained, isolated area to several days when multiple rooms or structural materials are involved.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and access control.** We confirm the area is safe to enter, coordinate with any responding agencies or property managers, and restrict access so family members and bystanders are not exposed.
2. **PPE and containment setup.** Crews suit up in protective gear and seal off the work area to prevent cross-contamination into unaffected parts of the property.
3. **Removal of affected materials.** Porous items that cannot be fully decontaminated, carpet, subflooring sections, certain furnishings, are removed and bagged according to biohazard handling practices.
4. **Decontamination and disinfection.** Remaining surfaces are cleaned with hospital-grade disinfectants and checked carefully before any area is cleared for normal use.
5. **Disposal and documentation.** Biological waste is packaged and disposed of in line with applicable handling requirements, and we document the work performed for insurance and property records.

## What separates a good trauma scene cleaning response from a bad one

The most common mistake in trauma scene cleaning is treating it like a standard deep clean: wiping surfaces and calling it finished while material underneath carpet, tile, or baseboards remains untreated. Odor that returns days later is usually a sign that contaminated material was cleaned over instead of removed. A thorough response also means correctly identifying which materials are salvageable and which need to come out entirely, since guessing wrong in either direction either wastes money or leaves a health hazard behind. Insurance adjusters reviewing these claims generally want clear photo documentation, an itemized list of removed materials, and disposal records, which is why we document each step as the work happens rather than after the fact.
