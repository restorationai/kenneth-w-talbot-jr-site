## What Crime Scene Cleanup actually involves

Forensic cleanup addresses what remains after law enforcement releases a scene: blood, bodily fluids, and other biological material that has penetrated flooring, walls, furniture, and sometimes subfloor and joists. The work covers homicide cleanup, suicide scene cleanup, and unattended death situations, each with its own pattern of contamination depending on how much time has passed and where the material traveled. Technicians work in full protective suits, respirators, and gloves, and they treat every surface as a biohazard until it has been tested and verified clean. Depending on the extent of saturation, affected materials like carpet, padding, or sections of drywall are often removed rather than treated, because porous materials that have absorbed biological fluid cannot be reliably sanitized. All waste is bagged, labeled, and disposed of according to biohazard waste regulations, not placed in regular trash.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and containment.** Before any cleaning starts, technicians identify the full extent of affected materials, including areas that may not be visibly obvious, such as subflooring beneath carpet or gaps behind baseboards, and seal off the work area to prevent cross-contamination.
2. **PPE staging and biohazard removal.** Crews suit up in protective gear rated for bloodborne pathogen exposure and remove contaminated materials, saturated porous items first, following OSHA-informed handling practices throughout.
3. **Cleaning and enzymatic treatment.** Remaining surfaces are cleaned with hospital-grade disinfectants and enzymatic solutions designed to break down biological residue that standard cleaners leave behind, including material that has soaked into seams and subfloor.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Biological decomposition produces odor compounds that linger in porous materials and HVAC pathways long after visible cleanup is done, so affected areas are treated separately from the disinfection step.
5. **Final verification and waste manifest.** Technicians do a final walkthrough, confirm no contaminated material remains, and complete the documentation required to show the waste was transported and disposed of properly.

## What separates a good crime scene cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating this like a deep-clean job instead of a biohazard remediation job. Surface cleaning without removing saturated subflooring or baseboards leaves contamination in place, and it will produce odor and liability problems weeks later. Another frequent miss is incomplete documentation: insurance carriers and property managers want to see what was removed, how it was disposed of, and proof the work followed a defined protocol, not just a verbal assurance that "it's clean now." A careful operator checks beneath flooring, inside wall cavities where fluid may have traveled, and in HVAC returns near the affected room, rather than stopping at what's visible. Discretion also matters here in a way it doesn't on other restoration jobs: unmarked vehicles, respectful communication with family members, and coordination around funeral timelines or law enforcement release of the scene are part of doing this work responsibly.
