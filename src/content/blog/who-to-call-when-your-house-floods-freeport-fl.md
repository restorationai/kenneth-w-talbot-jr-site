## Is This an Electrical or Gas Emergency First?

Call your power company or 911 before anyone touches standing water if you see sparking outlets, a submerged electrical panel, or you smell gas. Floodwater and electricity do not mix, and a tripped breaker from the street is safer than guessing.

If water has reached an outlet, a water heater, or your HVAC unit, shut off power at the main breaker yourself only if the panel is dry and accessible. If it isn't, that's the utility's job, not yours. Walton County's own disaster guidance covers this exact scenario for residents dealing with storm and rain-driven flooding ([Walton County Disaster FAQs](https://mywaltonfl.gov/1514/Disaster-FAQs)).

Once the hazard is controlled, or if there was never one to begin with, move to the next call.

## Heavy Rain and Creek Flooding vs. a Burst Pipe: Does the Source Change Who You Call?

The source of the water changes your insurance call, not your first call for help. Either way, a water extraction crew needs to get there quickly. But rainwater pooling in from outside and groundwater rising through a slab are generally treated differently by insurers than a pipe that fails inside your walls.

Freeport sits on flat, low terrain near the Choctawhatchee Bay, and sustained rain or creek overflow can push water in through doors, foundation cracks, and low-grade yards faster than a typical afternoon storm. That kind of surface water intrusion is usually excluded from a standard homeowners policy and instead falls under a National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) policy, if you carry one ([FloodSmart.gov](https://www.floodsmart.gov/)).

A burst supply line, a failed water heater, or an overflowing washing machine is a different animal. That's typically a sudden, accidental loss your homeowners policy is built to cover, subject to your deductible and policy language. If you want the fuller breakdown of what's typically covered and what isn't, see our guide on [whether homeowners insurance covers water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/).

Either way, don't wait to figure out the insurance question before calling for extraction. Standing water does damage by the hour, not by the week.

## Why Does the Type of Floodwater Change What Gets Removed?

Floodwater from outside the home, including rising creek water, overflowing ditches, or surface water pushed in by heavy rain, is classified as Category 3 water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's considered grossly contaminated. That classification drives real decisions about what stays and what goes.

Category 3 water typically means porous materials like drywall, baseboards, and carpet padding that were in contact with the water need to come out rather than be dried in place, and surfaces that remain need antimicrobial treatment. This isn't about being overly cautious; it's about not leaving contamination sealed behind a wall. A burst pipe with clean supply water starts as Category 1 and is usually handled differently, though it can degrade if it sits too long before drying begins.

## Should You Document Before Cleanup Starts?

Yes, take photos and video of the water level, affected rooms, and damaged belongings before anything is moved or removed. Insurers and flood adjusters rely on this documentation, and it's much harder to reconstruct after the fact.

Walk through with your phone, get wide shots of each room and close-ups of specific damage, and note the time and date. If you're filing an NFIP claim, keep receipts for anything you have to discard, since flood policies often require proof of loss.
