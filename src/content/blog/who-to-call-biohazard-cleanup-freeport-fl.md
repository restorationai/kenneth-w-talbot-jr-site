## What counts as a biohazard in a home?

A biohazard is any material that could carry disease because it's contaminated with blood, bodily fluids, or human or animal waste. In a home, that usually means blood from an injury, death, or violent crime, bodily fluids from an unattended death, vomit or feces beyond what normal cleaning handles, used needles or other sharps, and in severe cases a sewage backup that's contaminated a large area.

In Walton County, the calls that need this kind of response are usually an unattended or natural death discovered after some time has passed, a suicide, an accidental death, or the aftermath of a violent crime. Hoarding situations sometimes cross into biohazard territory too, when animal waste, spoiled food, or human waste has built up over months or years.

## Should you call 911 or the cleanup company first?

Call 911 or the local police first if this involves a crime, a suicide, or any death where the cause isn't already known and documented. Law enforcement or the medical examiner has to process and release the scene before any cleanup company can legally start work there.

Once the scene is released, that's when you call a biohazard cleanup provider. If the situation is a known accidental spill or a death that's already been fully processed by authorities (for example, hospice at home), you can go straight to a cleanup company. When you're not sure which situation you're in, call 911 and let them tell you.

## Why shouldn't you clean blood or bodily fluids yourself?

Blood and bodily fluids can carry bloodborne pathogens like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV, and household cleaners aren't designed to neutralize them. [OSHA's bloodborne pathogens guidance](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) exists specifically because exposure risk is real, even from fluids that look dry or old.

There's also a practical problem: porous materials like carpet padding, subfloor, and drywall absorb contamination in ways you can't fully clean with a mop and a bottle of bleach. A trained crew knows what has to be removed versus what can be disinfected and kept, which saves homeowners money versus tearing out more than necessary. Many reputable providers clean to [IICRC S540](https://iicrc.org/s540/), the industry standard for trauma and crime scene cleanup, even when they don't personally hold that certification.

## What does Florida's trauma scene cleanup registry actually mean?

Florida requires any company that transports biomedical waste itself to register with the Florida Department of Health under administrative rule 64E-16. That registration covers waste transport specifically, not a general license to perform cleanup work.

Some cleanup companies hold that registration directly. Others partner with a licensed third-party biomedical waste hauler to move and dispose of contaminated materials, which is also compliant. Either way, ask the company how contaminated waste leaves your property and who is licensed to handle it. A straight answer, with a registration number or a hauler's name, is a good sign.

## What should you ask a biohazard cleanup company before they start?

Ask these questions before any crew enters the home: how will you dispose of contaminated waste and are you registered or working with a licensed hauler, what PPE does your crew wear, will you provide a written scope before starting, and will you communicate directly with my insurance adjuster or property manager.

Also ask how they handle odor after fluids have penetrated subfloor or framing, since that's often the difference between a job that's actually finished and one that smells wrong a month later. A company that answers these plainly, without dodging, is worth trusting with a job this sensitive.

## Will homeowners insurance cover biohazard cleanup?

Coverage depends heavily on the cause and your specific policy, so this isn't something to assume either way. A death, crime, or accident that happens inside a covered home is sometimes covered under the dwelling or liability portion of a standard homeowners policy, per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), while situations like long-term hoarding buildup often aren't.

Get a written scope of work and keep every receipt and photo. That documentation is what an adjuster needs to process a claim, and a company that routinely works trauma scenes will know how to put that paperwork together correctly.
