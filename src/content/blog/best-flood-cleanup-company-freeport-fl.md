## How Fast Should a Flood Cleanup Crew Be on Site?

Faster is better, but the honest benchmark is hours, not minutes, and no company should promise an exact arrival time without knowing your location and the storm conditions that day. What matters more is whether the phone gets answered 24/7 and whether extraction starts the same day water enters the home.

Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA's flooded-homes guidance](https://www.epa.gov/flooded-homes), so the gap between the water entering your home and a crew starting extraction is the single biggest factor in how much drywall, flooring, and insulation you end up losing. Veterans Remediation & Restoration takes calls around the clock, which matters most for floods that happen overnight or during a weekend storm, when a lot of companies simply don't pick up until Monday.

Standing water also keeps spreading contamination the longer it sits, which is the next thing worth understanding before you pick a company.

## Why Does Category 1 vs. Category 3 Floodwater Change the Job?

Floodwater that enters a home from outside, whether it's rising bay water, storm surge, or surface runoff pooling against the foundation, is classified as Category 3 (black water) under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry reference for professional water damage restoration. That's a different job than a clean supply-line leak.

Category 1 water starts out clean, like a broken water heater line, but even clean water turns contaminated the longer it sits against drywall, carpet, and subfloor. Category 3 water is already contaminated when it arrives, so porous materials it touches, carpet pad, baseboards, lower drywall, usually get removed rather than dried in place, and the crew works in gloves and respiratory protection rather than just rubber boots.

This is also why a flood cleanup estimate for a Category 3 loss looks different from a typical burst-pipe estimate: more material removal, antimicrobial treatment, and a longer drying window before anything gets put back.

## What Should a Flood Cleanup Estimate Include, and What Should Raise a Red Flag?

A legitimate flood cleanup estimate lists extraction, the specific materials being removed (a "flood cut" is drywall removed a foot or two above the waterline so the wall cavity can dry), the drying equipment being placed, and a plan for how drying progress gets measured and recorded.

That last part matters more than most homeowners realize. Daily moisture readings, called drying logs, are what your insurance adjuster uses to confirm the structure actually dried before anyone closes the walls back up. A company that can't describe how they document drying is a company that's going to leave you arguing with your adjuster later. Our breakdown of [typical water damage restoration costs in Freeport](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost/) shows what these line items usually run.

Red flags to watch for, especially in the days after a big regional rain event when out-of-town crews show up looking for work: demands for full payment in cash before any work starts, no local address or license information they're willing to share, pressure to sign a contract on the spot, and no mention of moisture documentation at all. A company confident in its work will walk you through the scope before asking you to sign.

Before any cleanup starts, know what your policy actually covers. Standard homeowners insurance typically excludes flood damage from rising water or storm surge; that requires a separate policy through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood carrier, as explained on [FloodSmart.gov](https://www.floodsmart.gov/get-insured/buy-a-policy). Interior water damage from a sudden plumbing failure is a different coverage question, one our [water damage insurance guide](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) covers in more detail.
