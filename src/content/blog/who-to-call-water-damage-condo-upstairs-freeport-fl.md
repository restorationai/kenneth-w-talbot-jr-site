A leak from the unit above is its own kind of headache. It's not just your ceiling, it's a coordination problem between your unit, the owner upstairs, possibly the HOA, and two separate insurance carriers. Here's the order to make the calls in.

## Water Is Coming Through the Ceiling Right Now: What to Do First

Shut off power to any light fixture or outlet below the wet spot, move anything valuable out from underneath, and put down towels or a bucket, then call for drying help before the water sits long enough to soak the drywall core and the insulation above it.

Don't poke or puncture a sagging ceiling unless water is actively pooling behind it and about to fall on its own; a controlled release into a bucket beats an uncontrolled collapse, but this is a judgment call, not a rule to force.

Knock on the upstairs unit's door or call the owner if you have a number. If no one answers and water is still running, that's when the HOA or on-site property manager needs a call, since they may have a master key or an emergency contact for that unit. None of this replaces getting a drying crew started. Water sitting in a ceiling cavity for more than 24 to 48 hours creates the conditions mold needs to start growing, per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home).

## Who Is Responsible for Water Damage in a Florida Condo?

Responsibility usually comes down to where the water originated and what the condo's governing documents say a unit owner versus the association is responsible for maintaining. This isn't something a restoration company or a blog post can decide for you.

Florida condo associations are governed in large part by Chapter 718 of the Florida Statutes, which sets baseline rules for maintenance and insurance responsibilities, but every association's declaration can assign specifics differently. If the leak came from a supply line or appliance inside the upstairs unit, that owner's policy is typically the first stop. If it came from a common-element pipe in the building's structure, the association's master policy may apply instead. If there's any dispute, that's a conversation for the HOA board, the property manager, or an attorney, not something to sort out standing in a wet hallway. What you can control in the moment is getting the water out and the materials dried before the responsibility question slows anything down.

## Should You Call the HOA, the Upstairs Owner, or a Restoration Crew First?

Call the restoration crew first for the water itself, the HOA or property manager for access and common-element questions, and the upstairs owner to find out what happened and get their insurance information. These calls can happen almost simultaneously, but drying doesn't wait on any of them.

A plumber is only the right first call if water is still actively running from a fixture in the unit above and someone there can let a technician in to shut it off. If the source is already stopped, your time is better spent getting moisture readings started below. Veterans Remediation & Restoration handles the extraction, containment, and drying side of this; if the leak itself needs a repair upstairs, that's a separate call the upstairs owner or the association makes to their own plumber or contractor.

Your own insurance carrier comes in after mitigation starts, not before. Most policies want to see that reasonable steps were taken to limit the damage, which is part of why documenting everything from the first hour matters, as the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) explains for standard homeowner policies.

## What Happens When You Call a Restoration Company About an Upstairs Leak?

Expect a short phone screen about where the water is coming from and how long it's been wet, followed by a technician checking moisture levels in the ceiling, walls, and flooring before any drying equipment goes in. Have your unit number, the upstairs unit's number if known, and your HOA or property manager's contact ready.

On arrival, the crew maps the wet area with a moisture meter, photographs the damage for your records, and sets up air movers and a dehumidifier to bring the ceiling and wall cavities back to a dry standard, generally following the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage drying standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). If moisture has been sitting long enough to raise mold concerns, that inspection happens at the same visit rather than as a separate trip later.

## How Do You Document This for Two Insurance Claims?

Photograph the wet ceiling, walls, and any damaged contents from multiple angles before anything is moved or dried, then keep every receipt and the written drying report from the restoration crew. Both your carrier and the upstairs owner's carrier (or the association's, depending on the source) may ask for the same moisture readings and photos, so one clean set of documentation does the work for both claims.
